# Speak: an evidence-based A1–A2 scenario ladder

Research note for the Speak app (Hindi, French, Mandarin). Prepared 12 October 2026.

**How to read this note.** Each claim carries a tag:

- **[Established]**: a primary source or peer-reviewed research states it directly.
- **[Practice]**: common, reasonable practice in courses or syllabi. It is not tested research.
- **[Suggestion]**: my own proposal for Speak. Judge it on its merits.

---

## 1. Summary

1. **No authority sets a fixed topic order.** The Council of Europe level specifications list functions, notions and themes, but they do not sequence them. Courses share a broad order anyway: self, then people, then time, then getting things, then places, then past and future. [Established / Practice]
2. **The current ladder misses whole functions that A1 and A2 inventories list.** The main gaps are communication repair ("sorry, again?"), numbers and time, simple arrangements, invitations, transport, health, describing people, and talking about past events. [Established]
3. **Repair chunks should come first.** The CEFR says A1 interaction depends on repetition, slower speech and repair. The current ladder never teaches the learner how to ask for these. [Established / Suggestion]
4. **One meeting with a chunk is not enough.** Research on multi-word items shows clear gains only after many encounters (about 10–15 in incidental studies), and spaced practice beats massed practice. [Established]
5. **Nine scenarios cover about one fifth of what is needed.** An honest speaking strand for A1 needs about 20–24 scenarios. A2 needs about 24–28 more. [Suggestion, based on Established hour figures]
6. **Do not claim that Speak takes a learner "to A1" or "to A2".** Say that a stage practises the speaking side of A1 or A2 topics. [Suggestion]

## 2. What the inventories list for A1 and A2 speaking

### 2.1 CEFR (2001) and Companion Volume (2020)

**[Established]** The CEFR global scale describes A1 as follows: the learner "can introduce themselves and others and can ask and answer questions about personal details such as where someone lives, people they know and things they have. Can interact in a simple way provided the other person talks slowly and clearly and is prepared to help." For A2, the learner "can communicate in simple and routine tasks requiring a simple and direct exchange of information on familiar and routine matters" ([CEFR Companion Volume 2020, Appendix 1, p. 175](https://rm.coe.int/16809ea0d4); readable extract via [SLO mirror](https://www.slo.nl/publish/pages/18763/bijlage_1_companion_volume.pdf)).

**[Established]** The 2001 scale for overall spoken interaction says that at A1 "communication is totally dependent on repetition at a slower rate of speech, rephrasing and repair" ([CEFR 2001, p. 74](https://rm.coe.int/1680459f97)). This matters for Speak. Repair is not an extra. It is how A1 talk works.

**[Established]** The Companion Volume's appendix on salient features is the clearest guide to A2 content. It says A2 holds "the majority of descriptors stating social functions". Its examples include "greet people, ask how they are and react to news", "make and respond to invitations", "discuss what to do, where to go and make arrangements to meet" and "make and accept offers". A2 also covers "getting out and about": shops, post offices, banks, travel information, public transport, tickets and directions. The stronger A2+ band adds "describe habits and routines", "describe past activities and personal experiences", "describe plans and arrangements" and "explain what they like or dislike about something" ([CV 2020, Appendix 1, p. 173](https://www.slo.nl/publish/pages/18763/bijlage_1_companion_volume.pdf)).

**[Established]** The same appendix says A1 is "the lowest level of generative language use", above a stage that relies "purely on a very finite rehearsed, lexically organised repertoire of situation-specific phrases" (same source). The Companion Volume names that lower stage Pre-A1. A learner who can only perform learned chunks in fixed scenes is at Pre-A1. A learner who can recombine them for new needs is at A1. This is the honest test for Speak's labels (section 6.4).

The relevant Companion Volume scales are Overall oral interaction (p. 72), Conversation (p. 73), Goal-oriented co-operation (p. 76), Obtaining goods and services (p. 77), Information exchange (p. 78) and Sociolinguistic appropriateness (p. 136) ([CV 2020](https://rm.coe.int/16809ea0d4); searchable in the [CEFR descriptor database](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/cefr-descriptors-search)).

### 2.2 Breakthrough (A1) and Waystage (A2)

**[Established]** The draft Breakthrough specification (preface by John Trim, 2009) groups content into six function classes. These are factual information, attitudes, suasion ("getting things done"), socialising, structuring discourse and **communication repair**. It adds general notions (space, time, quantity, quality) and themes in four domains ([Breakthrough specification, EALTA copy](https://ealta.eu/documents/resources/Breakthrough%20specification.pdf)).

| Domain | Breakthrough themes |
|---|---|
| Personal | Personal identification; house, home and environment; daily life; free time, entertainment; relations with other people |
| Public | Public entertainment; travel; health and body care; shopping; food and drink; services |
| Vocational | Work and employment |
| Educational | School, study, classroom |

**[Established]** Breakthrough **does not fix an order** for these themes. It weights domains by learner group. Adults focus on the personal and public domains, and school learners on the personal and educational domains. It estimates **80–100 hours** of tuition to reach the level (same source).

**[Established]** *Waystage 1990* ([van Ek & Trim, CUP](https://www.casadellibro.com.co/libro-waystage-1990/9780521567077/805041)) uses the same function and notion design as *Threshold 1990*, at a lower level. The Companion Volume says A2 "does appear to reflect the level referred to by the 'Waystage' specification" ([CV 2020, p. 173](https://www.slo.nl/publish/pages/18763/bijlage_1_companion_volume.pdf)). I could not get a full open copy of Waystage. Its function lists below come through the CEFR text, not from the book directly.

### 2.3 French: *Niveau A1 / A2 pour le français* (Beacco et al.)

**[Established]** *Niveau A1 pour le français* (Beacco, Porquier et al., Didier, 2007) is a full referential. Its chapters cover functions (*actes de discours*), general notions, grammar, specific notions, sound, writing, cultural competence and strategies ([table of contents](https://www.obalkyknih.cz/file/toc/320502/pdf)). *Niveau A2* (Didier, 2008) follows the same plan. I could not open the full books. The lists below come from a university referential built on the CEFR.

**[Practice]** Aix-Marseille University's language service lists these spoken tasks ([SUL référentiel A1–B2](https://www.univ-amu.fr/system/files/media-ressource/SUL_R%C3%A9f%C3%A9rentiel%20des%20niveaux%20de%20langue%20A1%20%C3%A0%20B2.pdf)):

| Level | Spoken tasks |
|---|---|
| A1 | Introduce yourself and others; give name, age, nationality, job, address, phone; talk about family and leisure; express likes and dislikes; greet, invite, thank, say goodbye; **use *tu* or *vous* correctly**; accept or refuse an invitation; order, book and ask prices; say dates, times and habits; ask the way |
| A2 | Describe people (looks, character); talk about home, leisure and work; frequency and duration; directions and distance; request, propose, offer help; ask for and give advice; congratulate and give wishes; compare and justify choices; tell past events; talk about future plans |

Note that the French list puts ordering, booking and asking prices at A1, not A2.

### 2.4 Mandarin: HSK 3.0 standards (2021) and the 2025 syllabus

**[Established]** The *Chinese Proficiency Grading Standards for International Chinese Language Education* (MoE and State Language Commission, 2021; GF 0025-2021) set out nine bands in three stages ([Wikipedia summary with official sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanyu_Shuiping_Kaoshi)). Each band has topic-task content. The text below is from a copy of the standard ([Italian-hosted copy](https://www.tuttocina.it/HSK/NuovoHSK-lineamenti.pdf)).

| Band | Topics (话题) | Model tasks (任务) | Speaking target | 2021 word count |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Personal information, daily routine, food and drink, transport, hobbies | Use the simplest polite expressions with **different interlocutors**; read and check simple public signs | Answer simple questions with words and short sentences | 500 |
| 2 | Basic social contact, family life, study plans, **shopping, dining**, personal feelings | **Order at a Chinese restaurant with friends and talk about preferences**; fill in an enrolment form | Short Q&A, statements and social talk | 1,272 |

**[Established, but secondary sources]** A revised syllabus for HSK 1–6 was released on 15 November 2025. It lowers the word counts for the lower bands: 300 at level 1, 500 at level 2, and 1,000 at level 3. A global trial was held in January 2026, and full roll-out is planned for July 2026 ([Wikipedia, citing MoE and Chinese Test Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanyu_Shuiping_Kaoshi); [That's Mandarin](https://www.thatsmandarin.com/hsk/hsk-3-0-2026-update-what-chinese-learners-need-to-know/)). I could not open the 2025 syllabus itself. I have not checked whether its topic lists changed. The 2021 topics above are the safer reference.

**[Practice]** HSK bands do not map cleanly onto CEFR. Do not label HSK band 2 as "A2".

### 2.5 Hindi

**Little exists.** I found no CEFR-style referential for Hindi as a foreign language, and no frequency list of Hindi spoken chunks. What exists:

- **[Established]** The NCSSFL-ACTFL Can-Do Statements are language-neutral. At Novice level the learner "can communicate in spontaneous spoken... conversations on both very familiar and everyday topics, using a variety of practiced or memorized words, phrases, simple sentences, and questions" ([ACTFL](https://www.actfl.org/educator-resources/ncssfl-actfl-can-do-statements); [benchmark PDF copy](https://sites.wp.odu.edu/spanish101-hensley/wp-content/uploads/sites/32389/2023/04/Can-Do_Benchmarks_Indicators.pdf)). That phrase-based Novice profile fits a chunking app well.
- **[Practice]** Columbia's first-year Hindi outcomes say students can talk about themselves, family, interests, likes, dislikes and daily routine, and hold simple conversations about weather, meeting people, school and shopping ([Columbia Hindi-Urdu course descriptions](https://sai.columbia.edu/sites/sai.columbia.edu/files/content/courses/Hindi-Urdu%20Program%20Course%20Descriptions.pdf)). The list matches the CEFR A1–A2 themes closely.
- **[Practice]** *Elementary Hindi* (Delacy & Joshi, Tuttle 2014) builds its dialogues around everyday situations in India ([publisher](https://www.tuttlepublishing.com/books-by-country/elementary-hindi-paperback-with-disc)).

**[Suggestion]** For Hindi, use the CEFR functions and themes as the inventory, as most university programmes in effect do. Add India-specific scenes on top.

## 3. Order: is there a research-backed sequence?

**Short answer: no.** No study shows that one topic order beats another for beginners.

- **[Established]** Breakthrough leaves the order open and weights themes by learner need ([Breakthrough](https://ealta.eu/documents/resources/Breakthrough%20specification.pdf)).
- **[Established]** Michael Lewis replaced Present–Practice–Produce with Observe–Hypothesise–Experiment. But he "does not lay out instructional sequences" for the classroom ([Moudraia, ERIC Digest, 2001](https://cal.org/lexicalapproachtosecondlanguageteaching)).
- **[Practice]** Major beginner courses share a broad order anyway. Compare *Interchange 1* ([scope and sequence](https://www.cambridge.org/us/files/7615/0366/5204/Interchange_Level_1_Scope_and_Sequence.pdf)), *Integrated Chinese 1* ([lesson list](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/sfleming/chn201/ICTxtbk.html)), the HSK bands and the French A1/A2 lists:

| Common position | Interchange 1 | Integrated Chinese 1 | HSK bands 1→2 | French A1→A2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Me | Where are you from? | Greetings | Personal info | Introduce yourself |
| 2. People | Family (unit 5) | Family | Family life (2) | Family |
| 3. Time | Daily schedules (2) | Dates and time; appointments | Daily routine; study plans | Dates, time, habits |
| 4. Likes | Likes and dislikes (4) | Hobbies | Hobbies | Likes and dislikes |
| 5. Getting things | How much? (3); ordering (13) | Shopping; dining | Food (1); shopping, dining (2) | Order, ask prices (A1) |
| 6. Places | Neighbourhood (8) | Transport; directions | Transport (1) | Directions (A1/A2) |
| 7. Past | Past events (7, 10) | Birthday party | — | Past events (A2) |
| 8. Plans, health | Plans, invitations (15); health (12) | Seeing a doctor | Personal feelings (2) | Advice, plans (A2) |

**[Established, logic]** Three principles explain this shared order. All three come from the specifications, not from experiments:

1. **Personal before public.** The Breakthrough domains start with personal identification.
2. **Immediate need first.** The CEFR A1 profile centres on "areas of immediate need".
3. **Present before past and future.** A2+ adds past and plans, so they come late.

**[Suggestion]** Keep the shared order. Make one change backed by the CEFR: teach **repair** in the first stage. Move **ordering food** into A1. French A1 and HSK band 1 both place food transactions at the start.

## 4. Which chunks first

### 4.1 Frequency lists

- **[Established]** Shin and Nation (2008) list the most frequent collocations in spoken British English. The top items include *you know*, *I think*, *a bit*, *a lot of*, *thank you*, *very much*, *at the moment*, *a little bit* and *this morning*. Many of these are "meaningful, frequent units" fit for an elementary speaking course. The authors note one warning: greetings such as *good morning* do not reach the top 100, so **frequency alone does not decide usefulness** ([Shin & Nation, ELT Journal 2008](https://vuw-web.matrix.squiz.cloud/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/2008-Shin-Collocations.pdf)).
- **[Established]** Martinez and Schmitt's PHRASE List (*Applied Linguistics*, 2012) ranks 505 phrasal expressions from the BNC by frequency band ([Text Inspector summary](https://textinspector.com/help/analysing-vocabulary-using-the-phrase-list/)). It suits reading more than beginner talk.
- **Evidence gap.** These lists are for English. I found no beginner spoken-chunk frequency list for Hindi, French or Mandarin.

**[Suggestion]** Use the English lists for their **functions**, not their words. A beginner needs a hedge ("a bit"), an opinion opener ("I think"), quantity ("a lot of"), thanks, and time-now ("at the moment", "today"). Give each of these a natural equivalent in each language, and get it checked by a native speaker.

### 4.2 Formulaic language research

- **[Established]** Classroom beginners learning French in England first used rote chunks, then gradually "unpacked" them and reused the parts in new sentences. Rote learning and rule-building fed each other ([Myles, Hooper & Mitchell, *Language Learning* 1998](https://eprints.ncl.ac.uk/32092)). This is the best direct support for Speak's "chunks first, notice grammar last" design.
- **[Established]** Wray (2002) warns that adults tend to analyse rather than store whole phrases. She also doubts that a full phrase syllabus is workable, because the number of possible phrases is huge ([Cobb's review of Wray](https://www.lextutor.ca/cv/wray_review_2003.pdf); [Wray, CUP](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/formulaic-language-and-the-lexicon/formulaic-sequences-in-the-second-language-acquisition-process-a-model/72B0322A449268CEA5709D93A3A63A2F)). So keep each scenario's chunk set small and functional.
- **[Established]** Boers and Lindstromberg's review finds that a large repertoire of formulaic sequences helps learners, but that learners close the gap with native speakers slowly. Three kinds of treatment help: drawing attention to chunks, look-up work, and deliberate memorisation ([Boers & Lindstromberg, *ARAL* 32, 2012](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/annual-review-of-applied-linguistics/article/experimental-and-intervention-studies-on-formulaic-sequences-in-a-second-language/A2ACDF54604CFAC4443240748360C403)). Boers et al. (2006, *Language Teaching Research* 10/3) found that students taught to notice chunks were rated more proficient in oral interviews ([replication call, *Language Teaching*](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/abs/replication-research-in-pedagogical-approaches-to-spoken-fluency-and-formulaic-sequences-a-call-for-replication-of-wood-2009-and-boers-eyckmans-kappel-stengers-demecheleer-2006/8D7676F9CA677D380F6B3870CE20DA9F)).
- **[Established]** Lewis names the unit types as polywords, collocations, institutionalised utterances, and sentence frames and heads ([Moudraia 2001](https://cal.org/lexicalapproachtosecondlanguageteaching)). Speak's three types (fixed phrases, frames, collocations) map onto these.

### 4.3 Which spoken chunks give beginners the most use

**[Suggestion, grounded in the sources above]** Choose chunks by a three-part test:

1. **Function coverage.** Does the chunk serve one of the six Breakthrough function classes? Repair and socialising are the most neglected.
2. **Transfer.** Will the chunk reappear in at least three later scenarios? Examples: *please*, *thank you*, *I'd like ___*, *what time ___?*, *me too*, *sorry, again?*
3. **Frame value.** Prefer a frame with a slot ("I'd like ___", "Where is ___?") over a one-off phrase. One frame serves many scenes.

The highest-value beginner set is the **repair kit**: *Sorry?*, *Again, please*, *More slowly, please*, *I don't understand*, *How do you say ___?*, *What does ___ mean?* It meets the CEFR A1 condition directly, it serves every later scenario, and the current ladder does not teach it.

## 5. Recycling: how often must a chunk come back?

### 5.1 What the research shows

| Study | Finding | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| Webb, Newton & Chang (2013), *Language Learning* 63 | Collocations met 1, 5, 10 or 15 times in a graded reader. The 15-encounter group gained most on all tests ([summary in Macis 2018](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7716a95b-7425-4809-a54f-9f3a02d57755/content)) | Established |
| Peters, Puimège & Szudarski (2023), *Language Learning* 73(4) | Multisite replication: repetition still helps, but gains are smaller and the number of repetitions needed differs ([abstract](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/output/26531738/repetition-and-incidental-learning-of-multiword-units-a-conceptual-multisite-replication-study-of-webb-newton-and-chang-2013)) | Established |
| Szudarski & Carter (2016); Pellicer-Sánchez (2017) | Gains at 6–12 encounters; no clear difference between 4 and 8 encounters ([Macis 2018 review](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7716a95b-7425-4809-a54f-9f3a02d57755/content)) | Established |
| Webb, Uchihara & Yanagisawa (2023), *Language Teaching* 56(2) | Meta-analysis: spaced exposure beat massed exposure, and the gap was larger on delayed tests (g = 1.71 vs 0.58) ([Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/E38E3468FD2090B1FA3051051DE8E70C)) | Established |
| Yamagata, Nakata & Rogers (2023), *SSLA* 45 | Collocations practised weekly over three weeks beat massed practice on later tests, though in-session scores were lower ([PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/AFDA91E8ECFDB03151056108E0341FA3/S0272263122000225a.pdf/effects-of-distributed-practice-on-the-acquisition-of-verb-noun-collocations.pdf)) | Established |
| Serrano (2022), *SSLLT* 12(3) | Review of distribution-of-practice effects in L2 learning ([abstract](https://diposit.ub.edu/items/75c45f38-aefd-46bb-8cab-19fb8f93050e/full)) | Established |

**Caveats.** Most encounter-count studies test incidental learning from reading. Speak uses deliberate speaking practice with retrieval, which usually needs fewer encounters. No study gives a "magic number" for spoken chunks. The firm points are that one class is not enough, more encounters help, and spacing beats cramming.

### 5.2 A practical rule for Speak

**[Suggestion]**

1. **Home scenario.** A core chunk is met about 8–10 times in its own class (Hear it, Chunks, Echo, Role play, Frame drill, Free practice). This is massed practice. It feels good in class but fades.
2. **Rule of three returns.** Each core chunk must reappear, in use, in at least **three later scenarios** at growing gaps:
   - in the **next** scenario or the one after (about 1 week later);
   - **2–4 scenarios** later (about 2–4 weeks);
   - in the **stage review** scenario, then again in the next level.
3. **Target.** About **15 meaningful encounters across at least four separate class days**. This is in line with Webb et al. (2013), and the spacing results above support the pattern.
4. **Repair and politeness chunks** appear in every scenario from A1.1 onward.
5. **Add a retrieval warm-up.** Open each class with 3 minutes of recalling earlier chunks *before* learners hear them. Spec rule 6 applies: this changes the class steps, so it needs a spec change first.
6. **Track it in data.** Tag each chunk with a stable id. Have `check_speak.py` report chunks with fewer than three later reuses.

## 6. Volume: how many scenarios?

### 6.1 Guided hours

| Source | A1 | A2 | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Cambridge English](https://support.cambridgeenglish.org/hc/en-gb/articles/202838506-Guided-learning-hours) | 90–100 | 180–200 | Cumulative, all skills; "a guideline only" |
| [Alliance Française Leeds](https://afleeds.org.uk/faq) | 60–100 | 160–200 | Cumulative, one AF centre, French |
| [Breakthrough spec](https://ealta.eu/documents/resources/Breakthrough%20specification.pdf) | 80–100 | — | Tuition hours |

### 6.2 Hindi and Mandarin relative to French

| Language | FSI category | Class hours to professional level | Ratio to French |
|---|---|---|---|
| French | I | 600–750 | 1× |
| Hindi | III | about 1,100 | about 1.5–1.8× |
| Mandarin | IV | 2,200 | about 3× |

Source: [US State Department, FSI](https://www.state.gov/foreign-language-training). **Caveat.** FSI figures measure intensive adult study to ILR level 3, not to A1. Nobody has measured whether the ratio holds at beginner level. Much of Mandarin's cost lies in reading characters, which Speak does not teach, while tones are a real early speaking cost. Treat the ratios as a direction, not a formula.

### 6.3 Scenario count

**[Suggestion]** The reasoning:

- A1 takes about 60–100 guided hours across all four skills. Spoken interaction is one strand, perhaps a quarter to a third of the time. That gives about **20–30 hours** of speaking work for A1. A2 adds about the same again.
- One scenario takes 30–60 minutes. Recycling and review need more time on top.
- Coverage: the A1 lists above name about 20 distinct functions and scenes. A2 adds about 25 more.

**Result: about 20–24 scenarios for A1 and 24–28 for A2, so about 45–50 in all.** The current nine are about one fifth of that.

For Hindi and Mandarin, keep the **same scenarios** but expect more class time per scenario. A Mandarin scenario may need two sessions: one for tones and Echo, and one for Role play and the Frame drill. Do not build three separate ladders. A shared ladder keeps the content comparable and the build cost low.

### 6.4 Labels without overclaiming

**[Suggestion]**

- Do not write "Reach A1" or "A2 course". Speak trains one strand, scripted spoken interaction, and the CEFR level describes the whole learner.
- Write: **"Stage 3 · practises A1 speaking topics"**. Or use CEFR-style can-do goals: "I can ask the time and say when I am free."
- Mark the first stage **"Pre-A1 → A1"**. The Companion Volume describes learned, situation-bound phrases as below A1.
- For Mandarin, do not equate stages with HSK levels. For Hindi, add a note that no official Hindi A1/A2 referential exists.

## 7. Proposed ladder

**[Suggestion]** Ids are short and stable. ★ marks the nine existing scenarios, kept as built. Two of them move: ordering moves from A2 to A1, and small talk becomes the past-events anchor at A2. Chunks are given in English; each language needs a natural rendering checked by a native speaker.

Register key: **R** = good register practice (Hindi aap/tum/tu, French vous/tu, Mandarin 您/你). **G** = good gender practice (Hindi verb and adjective agreement, French adjective and past participle agreement).

## 8. Open points for the owner

1. **Spec change.** The retrieval warm-up (5.2) and the review scenario type both need a spec change before any code. Spec rule 6 applies.
2. **Native-speaker checks.** Every chunk above is an English gloss. The natural Hindi, French and Mandarin forms need review before content leaves "draft".
3. **Build order.** If time is short, build the gaps in this order: a1-repair, a1-time, a1-free, a1-price, a2-invite, a2-arrange, a2-trip, a2-transport. These cover the largest missing CEFR functions.
4. **Weakest evidence.** The parts with the thinnest support are the Hindi inventory (none official), the 2025 HSK topic lists (not seen first-hand), and the exact repetition target (no study tests scripted speaking practice). Treat those parts as provisional.
